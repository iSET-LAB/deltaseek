\begin{abstract}
As-built verification asks whether a building matches its model. Existing
pipelines collect data along preset or coverage-driven routes and compare
offline, so the verification objective never informs where the robot looks. We
ask whether making deviation detection the planning objective actually pays,
and we build the apparatus needed to answer that: an open benchmark that
derives Gazebo worlds from building models, injects deviations of known type,
location and magnitude at a controlled density, and scores a run by detections
per metre of \emph{drivable} traversal. On a Clearpath Husky A300 with a UR5e
and a wrist-mounted RGB-D camera, we compare a greedy information-gain planner
that selects both base poses and arm configurations against coverage, frontier
and goal-directed baselines. Three findings stand out. Measuring distance
around walls rather than in straight lines reverses the ranking, so the
accounting is not a detail. Under that honest accounting, greedy
information-gain planning does \emph{not} beat systematic coverage. Including
the manipulator in viewpoint selection helps every planner that stops to look,
by up to $9.3$ percentage points. We release the benchmark so that the second
finding can be attacked directly.
\end{abstract}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{INTRODUCTION}

Building Information Models (BIM) are increasingly used as a geometric prior
for construction robots \cite{halder2023review}. A natural application is
as-built verification: driving a sensor through a partially finished building
and reporting where reality departs from the model. Established pipelines
capture along a preset or coverage-driven route and register against the model
afterwards \cite{bosche2010,turkan2012,golparvar2015}. The verification
objective therefore never influences where the robot goes.

Making that objective drive the plan is an appealing idea, and it is the one we
set out to test. A robot that knows which elements it has already resolved
should be able to spend a limited traversal budget where uncertainty is
highest, rather than sweeping a floor uniformly. Including a manipulator adds a
second lever: an arm reaches vantage points a fixed sensor cannot, looking
beneath assemblies and into equipment enclosures without repositioning the
base.

Testing the idea required more infrastructure than the idea itself: no shared
benchmark supplies exact deviation ground truth at a controllable density, an
observation model fast enough for a planner loop, and a traversal metric that
accounts for walls. This paper contributes that apparatus and reports what it
says.

\textbf{Contributions.}
(i) An open, reproducible benchmark: element manifests keyed by IFC
\texttt{GlobalId}, seeded deviation sampling at a chosen density, matched
Gazebo worlds and exact labels, and a geometric observation model costing
$\sim$12\,ms per candidate viewpoint.
(ii) A viewpoint-planning formulation over base poses \emph{and} arm
configurations, with camera pose derived from the generated robot description
so the sensor mount cannot silently diverge from the robot.
(iii) An evaluation showing that traversal accounting reverses planner
ranking, that greedy information gain does not beat coverage under drivable
distance, and that the manipulator helps every planner tested.

\section{RELATED WORK}

\textbf{As-built verification.} Bosch\'e \cite{bosche2010} registers laser
scans against CAD objects for dimensional compliance; Turkan et al.
\cite{turkan2012} and Golparvar-Fard et al. \cite{golparvar2015} track progress
by comparing captured data with 4D models. These are offline comparisons of
data gathered along routes chosen for coverage, not for the questions the
comparison will ask.

\textbf{Active perception.} Next-best-view planning dates to Connolly
\cite{connolly1985} and remains active for volumetric reconstruction and
exploration \cite{bircher2016,isler2016}. Frontier exploration
\cite{yamauchi1997} maximises newly observed free space. These objectives
reward seeing \emph{more}, whereas verification rewards resolving specific
modelled elements. Recent construction work does close the perception--action
loop, but treats model--reality divergence as a safety signal while reaching a
navigation goal rather than as the objective itself \cite{TODOactive}.

\textbf{Budgeted selection.} Choosing viewpoints under a travel budget is an
orienteering problem \cite{golden1987}; greedy benefit-per-cost is the standard
heuristic and carries guarantees when the objective is submodular
\cite{nemhauser1978,krause2008}. Our results suggest the greedy \emph{routing},
not the objective, is what limits performance here.

\section{BENCHMARK}

\subsection{Scenes and deviations}

An adapter converts IFC products into a manifest of elements, each retaining
its \texttt{GlobalId}, IFC class, pose and geometry (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}).
Because that adapter currently approximates each product by its world-aligned
bounding box, a wall with a door becomes a solid barrier, which inflates
occlusion exactly where a viewpoint planner's reasoning lives. For the
experiments here we therefore generate a synthetic storey from primitives: a
central corridor with rooms down both sides, with walls emitted as jambs and a
lintel so that a doorway is a real opening. The default layout has 110
elements. Contents sit at heights that reward different arm postures: services
near the ceiling, panels at chest height, and fittings tucked against the far
side of equipment.

A seeded sampler injects five deviation types --- \texttt{displaced},
\texttt{rotated}, \texttt{resized}, \texttt{missing} and \texttt{unexpected}
--- at a specified density, defined as the fraction of nominal elements
carrying a deviation. Unexpected elements are counted separately, since they
add to the scene rather than modifying an element. A scenario identifier and
seed reproduce a benchmark exactly.

\subsection{Observation model}

Rendering every candidate viewpoint is infeasible when a planner scores
thousands of them, so we answer the cheaper question a planner actually asks:
what fraction of an element's surface would this camera see? Elements are
treated as oriented boxes and their surfaces sampled; a sample counts when it
lies inside the view frustum, faces the camera within an incidence limit, and
is unoccluded by another element. Evaluating one viewpoint against 110 elements
costs about 12\,ms.

Each deviation type is revealed by a different observation. A
\texttt{missing} element is found by seeing \emph{into} its modelled volume and
finding it empty; \texttt{rotated}, \texttt{resized} and \texttt{unexpected}
elements by observing the as-built element; a \texttt{displaced} element by
either. A deviation counts as detected the first time its governing
observation exceeds a visible-fraction threshold, which stands in for detector
sensitivity.

This model assumes perfect recognition and localisation, so it measures
\emph{viewpoint quality} rather than perception robustness: the right
separation for studying where to look, and a limitation for hardware
transfer.

\subsection{Traversal metric}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_scene.pdf}
  \caption{Plan view of the synthetic storey with one sampled scenario. Grey
  rectangles are elements, light grey are overhead services, red markers are
  injected deviations, and the blue path is the tour selected by the
  deviation-seeking planner under a 60\,m drivable budget. The planner
  concentrates on the corridor and enters comparatively few rooms.}
  \label{fig:scene}
\end{figure}

Detections per unit distance is the quantity we want to improve, so the
denominator must be a distance the robot could actually drive. We compute it on
an inflated occupancy grid using the platform's own Nav2 settings
\cite{macenski2020}: a 0.06\,m costmap resolution and the
$0.495\times0.349$\,m footprint, inflated by its 0.606\,m circumscribed radius.
The platform's global planner is NavFn, which is Dijkstra over a costmap, so we
compute that quantity directly rather than through an action server; a round
trip per query would make evaluation intractable. Treating inflated cells as
lethal bounds the planner's answer from above.

The choice is not cosmetic. On this storey, drivable distance averages $1.8\times$
the straight-line distance and reaches $11\times$.

\section{PLANNING AND BASELINES}

Candidate viewpoints pair a base pose, drawn from a grid of navigable free
space, with one of five inspection arm postures. Camera pose comes from
forward kinematics over the generated robot description, so the arm mount and
camera transform cannot diverge from the robot; we verified the chain against
the running simulator's TF tree to machine precision. The fixed-sensor
configuration is the same generator with the arm frozen, so the ablation
compares like with like.

Every planner sees only the nominal model. Ground truth is used afterwards, by
the scorer, which is what prevents a planner from quietly cheating.

The \emph{deviation-seeking} planner maintains an independent Bernoulli belief
over each element's deviation state. A viewpoint is worth the expected entropy
it removes, where an observation's resolving power ramps from zero below the
detection threshold to one when the element is properly seen. Viewpoints are
chosen greedily by benefit per unit distance under a traversal budget.

Baselines are \emph{coverage} (visit every base pose in a nearest-neighbour
sweep), \emph{frontier} (greedily reveal unseen area \cite{yamauchi1997}), and
\emph{goal-directed} (drive to a distant goal, observing only from poses along
the way). All four share the same candidate set, budget and cost function.

\section{EXPERIMENTS}

We evaluate in Gazebo on a Clearpath Husky A300 with a UR5e and a
wrist-mounted RealSense D435, configured through the vendor's own
\texttt{robot.yaml} generator so the same description serves simulation and
hardware. Scenes use the 110-element storey with deviations sampled at density
$0.2$ plus a $0.05$ unexpected rate, giving 28 deviations for the seed shown.
Each planner receives a 60\,m budget and a 5\,m usable depth range, and we
report the mean over five deviation seeds.

\subsection{Traversal accounting changes the answer}

Table~\ref{tab:main} and Fig.~\ref{fig:curves} report the same runs under both
cost models. Under straight-line distance the deviation-seeking planner leads
coverage by 2.1 points, which is inside one standard deviation. Under drivable
distance it trails by 6.4 points. The goal-directed baseline loses 17 points
when walls are charged for, because a straight run between distant poses is
precisely what a building does not allow.

We consider this the most transferable result in the paper: a
detections-per-metre claim is only as good as the metre, and straight-line
distance systematically flatters planners that wander.

\subsection{Greedy information gain does not beat coverage}

Under honest accounting, coverage detects $70.0\pm5.3$\% against
$63.6\pm6.1$\% for the deviation-seeking planner. The gap is stable across
seeds.

Fig.~\ref{fig:scene} suggests why. The planner concentrates on the corridor,
from which many elements are partially visible, and enters comparatively few
rooms; coverage systematically enters every one. Two mechanisms plausibly
contribute. First, with a uniform prior, maximising expected information about
every element's deviation state is close to asking to see every element, which
is what coverage does by construction --- the objective may simply not be
selective. Second, greedy benefit-per-metre is myopic: it repeatedly takes the
locally cheapest informative viewpoint and pays for detours it cannot amortise,
whereas a nearest-neighbour sweep is a serviceable travelling-salesman
heuristic. The second mechanism points at the routing rather than the
objective, and is the first thing we intend to fix.

\subsection{The manipulator helps every planner}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_ablation.pdf}
  \caption{Including the arm in viewpoint selection versus freezing it at one
  posture, under drivable distance. Every planner loses ground with a fixed
  sensor.}
  \label{fig:ablation}
\end{figure}

Freezing the arm costs the deviation-seeking planner 5.7 points
($63.6\rightarrow57.9$), coverage 6.4 ($70.0\rightarrow63.6$) and frontier 9.3
($47.9\rightarrow38.6$); the goal-directed baseline is nearly unaffected
because it barely stops (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}). In the selected plans the
robot routinely parks once and adopts several postures, gathering additional
observations for no traversal cost at all.

The honest reading is that the manipulator is valuable for as-built
verification in general, not for our objective in particular. That is a weaker
claim than we set out to make and a more useful one for practitioners choosing
a platform.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{LIMITATIONS AND NEXT STEPS}

The observation model is geometric; a depth-based detector behind the same
interface is required before any claim about perception robustness. Scenes are
synthetic, because the bounding-box IFC adapter would turn every doorway into a
wall; a tessellating importer is needed for real models. Traversal cost reasons
about walls, but the controller that executes a plan does not yet follow paths
around them. Results are five seeds on one layout at one density; the density
sweep the sampler was built for is not yet reported.

The immediate work is to replace greedy selection with tour construction and
local improvement, which tests the routing explanation directly. If the planner
still does not beat coverage, the uniform prior is implicated, and
deviation-seeking planning will need a defensible reason to prefer some
elements over others --- from construction sequence, trade handover, or
observed workmanship --- rather than treating all modelled elements as equally
suspect.

\section{CONCLUSION}

We built the apparatus needed to ask whether deviation-seeking viewpoint
planning pays, and used it to answer honestly: on this benchmark it does not
yet beat systematic coverage once traversal distance is measured around walls,
though including a manipulator in viewpoint selection helps every planner
tested. We release the benchmark, the baselines and the evaluation so that the
negative result can be attacked rather than repeated.
