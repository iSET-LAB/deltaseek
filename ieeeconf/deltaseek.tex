\begin{abstract}
Construction environments evolve continuously. Elements are installed,
temporary objects appear and disappear, materials become occluded, and
surfaces that were visible last week are inaccessible today. Robotic
perception in such settings cannot always be treated as a static mapping
problem in which observations are gathered first and interpreted afterward:
large geometric and temporal change reduces correspondence between
observations taken at different times, making registration and persistent
scene understanding progressively harder. This motivates a more active
formulation, in which a robot deliberately selects sensing configurations that
supply the information needed to resolve the current state of the environment.

We present DeltaSeek, an initial framework toward active perception in evolving
built environments. The broader objective is a robot that reasons about
\emph{where} to observe, \emph{how} to observe, and ultimately \emph{when}
enough evidence has been gathered. As a first step we study a question
underlying all three: how does sensing embodiment constrain the observations
available to a mobile robot? We build an IFC-derived simulation benchmark and
evaluate a Husky A300 with a UR5e under chassis-mounted and wrist-mounted RGB-D
configurations, scoring candidate observations by geometric visibility and
effort by drivable distance on the platform's navigation costmap.

In a room-scale environment containing eight controlled scene changes spanning
four observability conditions, an exhaustive evaluation over 240 admissible
base poses shows that two changes cannot be observed by the chassis camera from
\emph{any} tested viewpoint, while the wrist camera resolves both. Changes
constructed around occlusion and viewing incidence remain observable from the
chassis but demand less accessible viewpoints and more travel: median drivable
distance to first observation rises from $6.0$~m with wrist-mounted sensing to
$14.2$~m with chassis-mounted sensing. These results separate two effects that
matter for active perception --- \emph{sensing capability}, where embodiment
decides whether an informative observation exists at all, and \emph{acquisition
cost}, where the same information is available but must be paid for in motion.
We argue this distinction is a necessary foundation for robots that plan
perception rather than passively accumulate data.
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}

A construction site is not a scene to be mapped once. Between two visits, walls
are erected, services are hung, pallets are delivered and consumed, and
formwork is struck. The same physical space presents a materially different
surface geometry from week to week, and the parts that changed are precisely
the parts a verification system cares about.

Most robotic capture pipelines in construction are nevertheless organised
around passive accumulation: drive a coverage route, collect point clouds or
images, register them to the building model, and compute differences
offline~\cite{bosche2010,turkan2012,golparvar2015}. This is a reasonable
division of labour when the environment is nearly static. It degrades when it
is not. Registration relies on correspondence between what was seen before and
what is seen now, and large change is exactly the condition under which that
correspondence thins out. The information the robot most needs is the
information its pipeline is least equipped to acquire.

Active perception offers the alternative
framing~\cite{bajcsy1988,aloimonos1988}: treat sensor placement as a decision
that resolves a specific uncertainty. Applied to an evolving building, this
means asking where to look next to determine whether part of the model still
describes reality --- which requires answering, in sequence, where to observe,
how to observe, and when enough evidence has been gathered.

This paper takes the first step, and deliberately a narrow one. Before a
planner can choose among sensing configurations, we need to know what the
robot's embodiment makes available at all. A mobile manipulator can place a
camera almost anywhere within its reachable workspace; a fixed chassis sensor
cannot. That extra freedom is usually assumed to be valuable. We measure it.

Fig.~\ref{fig:platform} shows the setup and the effect it measures. Our
contribution is threefold. First, a reproducible IFC-derived benchmark that
clips a real Revit export into a room-scale scene and injects controlled
changes with exact ground truth. Second, an observability taxonomy that
classifies a change by \emph{why} it is hard to see rather than by what moved.
Third, an exhaustive sensor-configuration study whose central finding is a
distinction we believe generalises: embodiment separates changes into those a
sensor configuration cannot observe at all, and those it can observe only at
greater cost.

\section{RELATED WORK}

\textbf{As-built verification} against a building model is well established,
from dimensional compliance checking~\cite{bosche2010} to progress
tracking~\cite{turkan2012,golparvar2015}. These systems consume data rather
than plan it: the route is set by coverage or an operator, and the verification
objective never influences where the sensor goes.

\textbf{Active perception} treats sensing as an action to be
planned~\cite{bajcsy1988,aloimonos1988}. Next-best-view methods maximise
expected information for reconstruction~\cite{connolly1985,isler2016} or
exploration~\cite{bircher2016,yamauchi1997}, usually under an unknown-space
objective. Our setting differs: a strong prior exists, and the question is what
has \emph{changed}. Mounting the sensor on an arm enlarges the achievable
viewpoint set, but how much that is worth is rarely quantified.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{BENCHMARK}

\subsection{From IFC to a room-scale scene}

The benchmark derives its geometry from a real Revit~2025 IFC2X3~\cite{ifc}
export of a
single-storey building, $44.5 \times 19.0 \times 4.5$~m, comprising 133
products that convert without failure through IfcOpenShell~\cite{ifcopenshell}. Each product is approximated by a box
oriented in its own placement frame. Orientation is not a refinement: the
export sits on a site grid rotated $0.90^\circ$ off the world axes, and a
world-aligned box turns a $0.203$~m wall into a $0.82$~m one, a fourfold error
on the dimension that decides whether the robot fits between them.

A storey is the wrong unit for studying observation, and is unusable directly
--- one wall entity runs the full $39$~m of a facade. We clip a manifest to a
rectangle, cutting in each element's own frame so the rotation survives. The
result, Hall~B, is $7.0 \times 12.5$~m with twelve modelled elements. Two
fit-out units are added, since the export contains no furniture and both
occlusion and height-limited observability need something to stand behind or on
top of.

\subsection{Observation model}

Detection is geometric rather than image-based. An element's surface is
sampled, and a sample counts when it falls inside the view frustum, faces the
camera within a $75^\circ$ incidence limit, and is unoccluded by another
element. A change is observed when the visible surface fraction supporting it
exceeds $0.05$, which stands in for a detector's sensitivity. Each change type
carries its own evidence: an unmodelled object is revealed by seeing it, a
displaced element by seeing it in its new position or its modelled volume
empty.

The model assumes perfect recognition and localisation, so it measures
\emph{viewpoint quality} rather than perception robustness --- and it is fast
enough to evaluate exhaustively, which rendering is not.

\subsection{Effort}

Inspection effort is drivable distance: Dijkstra search over an occupancy grid
inflated by the platform's own Nav2 footprint ($0.606$~m circumscribed radius,
$0.06$~m resolution). This matters --- on a storey-scale scene drivable
distance averages $1.8\times$ the straight line and reaches $11\times$, and
straight-line distance would let a robot pass through walls to reach a
viewpoint.

\subsection{Observability classes}

A change's type says what moved; it says nothing about whether a sensor can see
it. We therefore label each change by \emph{why} it is hard to observe:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{trivial} --- a large unmodelled object in open floor space;
  \item \textbf{height} --- above the chassis camera's sightline;
  \item \textbf{incidence} --- resolvable only from an oblique angle;
  \item \textbf{occluded} --- in another object's shadow from most base poses.
\end{itemize}

Eight changes were hand-placed in Hall~B, two per class, in a committed
scenario rather than sampled, so that each is explainable from geometry and the
study is exactly reproducible. The \emph{trivial} pair is included as a
control: a configuration that misses it is visibly broken.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{SENSING CONFIGURATIONS}

The platform is a Clearpath Husky A300 with an AMP enclosure and a UR5e,
generated from a single \texttt{robot.yaml} so that simulation and hardware
share one description. It carries two RGB-D cameras of the \emph{same} model,
so the comparison isolates where a camera is mounted rather than how good it
is:

The \textbf{chassis} camera is fixed on the enclosure deck at
$(0.460, 0.000, 0.418)$~m in the base frame, pitched $0.17$~rad down, so its
pose depends only on the base. The \textbf{wrist} camera is eye-in-hand, its
pose obtained by forward kinematics over the generated description across five
inspection postures; that chain is verified against the running simulation's
transform tree to machine precision.

Candidate base poses are laid on a $1.0$~m grid of navigable free space inside
the room, giving \textbf{240 admissible base poses}. Each combines with the
five arm postures, so the wrist camera has $1200$ distinct poses while the
chassis camera has $240$ --- it does not move with the arm. An element is
observed if \emph{any} active sensor observes it.

\subsection{Capability: what cannot be seen at all}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports what each configuration observed within a $25$~m
budget, and Fig.~\ref{fig:result}(a) the underlying capability.

The headline is \emph{not} $5/8$. A single planned run conflates what a sensor
can see with what one route reached, so we separate them by evaluating every
admissible pose exhaustively. Two changes --- both \emph{height} --- are
observable from \textbf{none} of the 240 chassis poses, at zero visible
fraction: a property of the embodiment. One further change the chassis-only run
missed is observable from 14 of those 240 poses; the planner did not visit one
inside the budget, which is routing, not evidence for the manipulator.

The distinction is not academic: repositioning the arm on the deck during
development moved the chassis result from $6/8$ to $5/8$, which reads as the
manipulator becoming more necessary and was entirely routing noise. The
capability figure did not move.

Fig.~\ref{fig:reach} explains why only \emph{height} produces a capability gap.
Swept over all base yaws at the $5$~m range limit, the chassis camera reaches
$2.23$~m and the wrist camera $3.59$~m, against a ceiling at $2.44$~m. The
wrist-only band is therefore $0.21$~m tall, and the two \emph{height} changes
were placed in it at $2.25$~m.

\subsection{Acquisition cost: what is merely expensive}

The \emph{occluded} and \emph{incidence} pairs were designed to require the
manipulator and did not. The crate behind the pallet stack is observable from
16 of 240 chassis poses --- hidden from $93\%$ of the room, as intended --- but
a planner needs only one of the remaining sixteen. Where both configurations
succeed, the difference appears as distance rather than as detections
(Fig.~\ref{fig:result}(b)). Median drivable distance to first observation is
$6.0$~m for the wrist configuration and $14.2$~m for the chassis, a factor of
$2.4$.

Occlusion, the intuitive case for a manipulator, is in this environment a
routing cost paid in metres, not a sensing limit.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{DISCUSSION}

Two effects fall out of these measurements, and an active-perception system
must treat them differently. \textbf{Sensing capability} is binary and set by
embodiment: no planning recovers a change outside every achievable frustum.
\textbf{Acquisition cost} is continuous and set by the environment --- the
information exists, and the question is how much motion it takes. This is where
viewpoint planning has leverage, and where the $6.0$~m against $14.2$~m gap
lives. Conflating them is easy: a single run reports only ``observed'' or
``not observed'', so an expensive change is indistinguishable from an
impossible one.

The capability boundary is also a design variable. Hall~B's band is $0.21$~m
because the chassis camera sits at $0.418$~m pitched $0.17$~rad down; levelling
it would shrink the band toward zero, and a taller space would widen it. The
useful output is therefore not ``the arm is worth two changes'' but a method
for locating that boundary before committing to a design.

\section{LIMITATIONS}

The result is narrow: one room, one ceiling height, eight changes. The
$0.21$~m band is a property of this geometry, and a trend needs rooms of
differing height. Detection is geometric --- no detector, no renderer, no noise
--- so these numbers describe viewpoint quality, not whether a perception stack
would recover the change. Elements are boxes, discarding the 48 opening
entities in the source model, so Hall~B is a sealed room the robot must be
spawned inside. And the framework does not yet answer its third question:
deciding \emph{when} enough evidence has been gathered needs graded belief and
a stopping rule, not binary detection.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{CONCLUSION}

DeltaSeek is a first step toward robots that plan perception in environments
that will not hold still. Using an IFC-derived, reproducible benchmark we asked
what a mobile manipulator's embodiment buys. For a room-scale scene with eight
controlled changes the answer is sharper and smaller than the usual assumption:
two changes lie outside the chassis camera's reach entirely, and the rest cost
roughly $2.4\times$ the travel without the arm. A robot that will one day decide
when it has seen enough must first distinguish a view it cannot obtain from one
it has not yet paid for.
